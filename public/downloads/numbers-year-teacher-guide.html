Teacher Guide

The Numbers Year · Grade 5 · 36 weeks

Answers, the point of each week, and the specific wrong turn each one produces. Written for a teacher who has never run an enterprise program and does not intend to become an expert in one.

Where a question is a judgment call this guide says so rather than inventing a correct answer to mark against.

Grade 5 has far more arithmetic with one right answer than Grade 4 did, which makes this guide more useful and also more dangerous. Every number in it is recomputed from the problem as stated in the workbook by verify.py, which ships beside it.

The two sessions, and the rule about money

Weeks 29, 34Two selling sessions with one deliberate change in between. They do not need two separate days: two halves of one market with a break, two lunch periods, or two visiting classes all work. What matters is that students get to act on what they measured.
If only oneIf a second session is genuinely impossible, week 34 becomes a paper exercise: students write what they predict the change would have done, and why. It is weaker, and worth telling them so.
Play moneyReal products, real customers, real decisions.
Set these twoThe startup fund (the same for every student, around $20 in play money works) and a price list for the class supply table. Keep supply prices near the real cost of the materials so the arithmetic stays honest.
The loopBank lends the fund → student buys materials from the supply table → student sells and makes their own change → student repays the fund. What is left is profit.
Why play moneyIt is what lets students run their own money box, so making change becomes a skill they practice rather than a risk you manage. It also fixes the money in the room, which is what makes the week 30 market-size arithmetic possible.
RecordsEach student keeps their own live tally in the workbook. Weeks 32 and 33 are built on it, so a tally filled in afterwards corrupts two weeks of arithmetic.
Trimester 1 · Weeks 1 to 12

Measure it

The arithmetic of a business, worked on other people's numbers. Nothing of the student's own is designed until week 13, so the tools exist before there is anything to point them at. 5.NBT.7 does most of the work.

Week 1Money to the Penny

Measuring · sits beside: Adding and subtracting decimals to hundredths (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$8.88. 24 x $0.37.

The point

Cents are not a rounding detail. Over a few hundred sales they are the whole profit, so this year every cost, price and total is written to two places.

Answers and marking
  • a $1.65; b $1.35; c $5.40; d $1.20; e $3.56.
  • Round or exact: round is fine only for the first row, a rough go/no-go judgment. Everything else must be exact.
Watch for

Students drop the trailing zero and write $1.6 for $1.60, then misread it later as sixteen cents. Insist on two decimal places everywhere, including when the second one is a zero.

Challenge Zone

$0.03 x 1,000 x 52 = $1,560 a year. Three cents is not a small number once it is multiplied by everything. Students who reach a figure in the thousands have understood the point.

Week 2Two Kinds of Cost

Measuring · sits beside: Multiplying decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$17.00. 20 x $0.40 = $8.00, plus the $9.00 sign.

The point

Fixed costs are paid once whatever happens; variable costs happen again for every item. Grade 4 added them together, and almost everything in this year depends on separating them.

Answers and marking
  • Sorting: cardboard fixed, beads variable, borrowed table fixed, bag variable, printing 40 labels at once fixed.
  • Totals: 10 items $13.00; 25 items $19.00; 50 items $29.00.
  • Cost of one, all in: $1.30, $0.76, $0.58.
Watch for

Students call the bag fixed because it is small, or the table variable because it was used all day. Size and duration are irrelevant; the only test is whether making one more costs you it again.

Challenge Zone

Making more spreads fixed costs thinner, so the obvious answer is make thousands. The two real limits are that unsold stock is money thrown away, and that you have to pay for materials before anyone buys anything.

Week 3Cost Per Unit, Properly

Measuring · sits beside: Dividing decimals and whole numbers (5.NBT.6, 5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$0.15. $6.75 divided by 45.

The point

Divide the pack down to one before multiplying by how many you use. Then add the ones that came out wrong, because you paid for those too.

Answers and marking
  • Card $0.08 per sheet, 1 used, so $0.08. Ribbon $0.012 per cm, 12 cm, so $0.144, which rounds to $0.14. Ink $0.06 per print, 2 used, so $0.12.
  • Bag $0.06. Label $0.03.
  • Spoilage: cost of 10 divided by 9 sellable.
Watch for

Students multiply first and divide second, or divide by how many they use rather than how many are in the pack. Do one on the board slowly.

Challenge Zone

The big pack is $0.12 a sticker against $0.15, so cheaper per sticker. What they need to know first: whether they will use 200, whether they have $24 now, and whether stickers spoil. Cheaper per unit is not automatically better.

Week 4Margin

Money · sits beside: Multiplying fractions and finding a fraction of a quantity (5.NF.4, 5.NF.6)
Warm-up answer

Gap $1.40. As a fraction of $4.00 that is 7/20, which is 35%.

The point

Margin as a share of the price, not just the difference. This is what lets you compare products that cost completely different amounts.

Answers and marking
  • Bracelet margin $2.00, about two thirds of the price. Bag margin $1.00, one fifth. Badge margin $1.50, three quarters.
  • By dollars: bracelet, badge, card, bag. By share: badge, bracelet, card, bag. The top two swap.
Watch for

Students compute the gap and stop. Push for the share every time, because week 21 depends on it and so does every comparison in the second trimester.

Challenge Zone

$2.00 x 10 = $20.00 against $0.80 x 40 = $32.00, so the second product wins on total margin. What would change it: how long each takes to make, and whether you could actually sell 40.

Week 5Break Even

Money · sits beside: Dividing to solve problems (5.NBT.6, 5.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

16 sales. $24.00 divided by $1.50.

The point

Fixed costs divided by the margin on one. Below that number you have lost money. It is the first question any adult asks about a small business.

Answers and marking
  • $18.00 / $0.90 = 20. $30.00 / $2.50 = 12. $12.50 / $0.50 = 25.
  • $20.00 / $1.50 = 13.33, so 14. You round up because 13 sales does not cover the fixed costs and you cannot sell a third of an item.
Watch for

Students divide by the price instead of the margin, which makes break even look about three times better than it is. Watch for it every time.

Challenge Zone

Cutting the sign from $9.00 to $3.00 removes $6.00 of fixed cost, so break even falls by $6.00 divided by their margin. The counter-argument is that a worse sign may cost more sales than it saves, and there is no way to know without trying it. Both halves needed.

Week 6The Price Ladder

Money · sits beside: Multiplying decimals; patterns (5.NBT.7, 5.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

$3.00 x 30 = $90. $4.00 x 22 = $88. The lower price brings in slightly more.

The point

Raising a price gives you more per sale and fewer sales. Only arithmetic tells you which wins, and the best row is usually in the middle.

Answers and marking
  • Money in: $69.00, $80.00, $87.50, $90.00, $88.00, $60.00, $30.00.
  • Cost at $1.20 each: $55.20, $48.00, $42.00, $36.00, $26.40, $14.40, $6.00.
  • Profit: $13.80, $32.00, $45.50, $54.00, $61.60, $45.60, $24.00.
  • Money in peaks at $3.00. Profit peaks at $4.00. They are different rows, which is the whole point of the exercise.
Watch for

Students fill the money-in column, see it peak, and stop. The profit column is where the answer changes, so require both.

Challenge Zone

Two real ways to find out instead of guessing: ask the week 17 testers what they would pay, or run session one at one price and session two at another, which is exactly what week 33 sets up.

Week 7Reading a Sales Chart

Measuring · sits beside: Plotting ordered pairs on the coordinate plane (5.G.1, 5.G.2)
Warm-up answer

32. 4 + 6 + 5 + 9 + 8.

The point

Points on a grid show shape. A list of numbers shows a total. The shape is what tells you what to do next.

Answers and marking
  • Most in session 4, fewest in session 1. Biggest rise between sessions 3 and 4. Session 2 is the ordered pair (2, 6).
  • The second stall totals 32 as well, but climbs steadily rather than bouncing.
Watch for

Students plot up first and across second. Say 'along the corridor, then up the stairs' once and it usually sticks.

Challenge Zone

The second stall is the better bet for session 6 because it is trending up while the first is not. Accept any answer that argues from the shape rather than from the total.

Week 8The Average Sale

Measuring · sits beside: Dividing to find an average (5.NBT.6)
Warm-up answer

$24.00.

The point

Total divided by how many. Useful for planning, and it hides everything about how a day actually went.

Answers and marking
  • Average $4.00. To take $60 at that average you need 15 customers.
  • Both stalls average $4.00. Stall A is predictable; stall B depended on one customer spending $16.
Watch for

Students divide by the wrong number, usually by the number of items rather than the number of customers. Those are different when someone buys two.

Challenge Zone

Without the $16 customer, stall B averaged $1.60 over five customers. That is the lesson: an average over a handful of sales can be moved almost entirely by one of them.

Week 9Two Products, One Table

Money · sits beside: Operations with decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$48.50. (14 x $2.50) + (9 x $1.50) = $35.00 + $13.50.

The point

Once there is more than one product you need a row each, because the answer to 'which one is carrying us' is almost never the one that sold most.

Answers and marking
  • Cards: money in $35.00, margin each $1.40, total margin $19.60.
  • Badges: $13.50, $1.10, $9.90. Bags: $24.00, $2.20, $8.80.
  • Keyrings: $21.00, $0.45, $9.45.
  • Sold most: keyrings. Most money in: cards. Most total margin: cards. Most margin per sale: bags. Four questions, three different answers.
Watch for

Students compute money in and call it done. The total margin column is the one that pays them, and it is the column they skip.

Challenge Zone

Bags make $2.20 each against cards at $1.40. To beat the cards' $19.60 they would need 9 bags instead of 4. Whether that is realistic depends on how many people will pay $6.00, which the table does not answer.

Week 10Good Days and Bad Days

Measuring · sits beside: Line plots and interpreting data (5.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

8. The largest is 11 and the smallest is 3.

The point

Two businesses with the same average can need completely different amounts of stock. Range is what tells you that.

Answers and marking
  • Stall A range 2. Stall B range 12.
  • Stall A: bring about 7. Stall B: the honest answer is that no single number works, which is the point.
Watch for

Students want a rule for how much to bring. There is not one; the right answer depends on what happens to the unsold ones, which is week 10's closing question.

Challenge Zone

A business like stall B is anything with occasional big days: an ice cream stall, a stadium seller, a shop near a school on a half day. Owners deal with it by carrying stock that keeps, or by staffing up only on the big days.

Week 11Who Else Is Selling?

Measuring · sits beside: Fractions of a whole; comparing fractions (5.NF.1, 5.NF.2)
Warm-up answer

$480.00. 60 x $8.

The point

The money in the room is fixed and knowable, so a plan can be checked against it. Every stall is competing for slices of the same total.

Answers and marking
  • Total $480.00. An even share across 12 stalls is $40.00.
  • A $60 plan is one eighth of the room, which is one and a half times an even share.
Watch for

Students treat an even share as a target. It is a ruler. The useful move is asking what would justify planning above it.

Challenge Zone

Among five similar stalls an even share of their part is smaller again. Ways to be picked that are not price: being first in the walking route, looking better, being the only one with a certain thing, or knowing the customers.

Week 12The Numbers Detective

Review · sits beside: Review: decimals, division, margin, break even (5.NBT.6, 5.NBT.7, 5.NF.4)
Warm-up answer

$132.00. 80 x ($3.00 - $1.35).

The point

Everything from weeks 1 to 11 applied to one real business. Mark the reasoning, not the numbers.

Answers and marking
  • Entirely dependent on the business chosen. An estimate marked with a question mark and defended beats a confident invention every time.
  • Most students find five or six of the seven rows are guesses. That is the correct result and worth saying out loud.
Watch for

Students present guesses as facts. Require the question mark. It is a habit that matters far beyond this lesson.

Challenge Zone

Adding rent, power and the owner's time usually makes a small business look barely profitable. Students who reach 'the owner is paid last' have found something real about small business.

Trimester 2 · Weeks 13 to 24

Build the line

Teams of two or three, a line of three products cut down to two, and a plan ending in a written forecast. Fractions arrive properly here: in the profit split, and in margin as a share of the price.

Week 13Your Team, Your Roles

Building · sits beside: Adding and subtracting fractions with unlike denominators (5.NF.1)
Warm-up answer

1/4. 1 - 1/2 - 1/4.

The point

Decide roles, decision rights and the profit split in September, in writing, because none of the three can be agreed in June with money on the table.

Answers and marking
  • Splits must sum to one whole and students must show that they do. Equal thirds is 1/3 each. An unequal split needs a stated reason.
  • The two hard questions are the assessment. A team with no answer to 'who decides' has not finished.
Watch for

Teams split by whose idea it was. Redirect to work done, which is the only basis they will still agree with in June.

Challenge Zone

The argument for equal is that it prevents most disputes and nobody tracks hours accurately anyway. The argument against is that it can reward the partner who does least. Students who find the second harder to write have usually noticed it is about a real person in their team.

Week 14The Problem You Will Solve

Building · sits beside: Multi-step problems with decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

16. $30.00 divided by $1.85 is 16.2, so 16.

The point

One problem, one customer, and a customer reachable twice. The repeat requirement is new this year and it rules out some good ideas on purpose.

Answers and marking
  • Open. Reject anything where the student cannot name a place and a time they would find the customer a second time.
  • 'What they do about it now' is the most useful row. Nobody has an unsolved problem, only a bad current solution.
Watch for

Students arrive with a product and reverse-engineer a problem for it. The 'what do they do now' interviews expose it quickly.

Challenge Zone

If they cannot beat the existing solution on price, the two other things are usually convenience and how well it fits this particular customer. Both are real and both are cheaper to compete on than price.

Week 15Good, Better, Best

Building · sits beside: Multiplying fractions and decimals (5.NF.4, 5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$1.80. 1 and 1/2 times $1.20.

The point

Three versions at three prices for the same customer solving the same problem. Different customers, or different problems, means three businesses.

Answers and marking
  • The four checks are the assessment. Any 'no' has to be fixed before week 16.
  • What makes BEST better must be sayable in one word and visible in a second.
Watch for

Students design three unrelated products. The same-customer and same-problem checks catch it. Send them back rather than letting it through.

Challenge Zone

Making the middle option look obvious is done by pricing the cheap one close to it, or leaving out something the customer wants. This is a real technique and noticing it is the whole point of the question.

Week 16Build All Three Rough

Building · sits beside: Converting measurement units (5.MD.1)
Warm-up answer

12. 3 m is 300 cm, divided by 25 cm.

The point

Three rough versions this week, timed and measured, so that week 18 has evidence to cut on.

Answers and marking
  • Times must be measured with a clock. Most students are out by about half.
  • In an hour: 60 divided by the minutes each, rounded down.
Watch for

Students polish one product instead of roughing three. Set a time limit out loud and hold to it.

Challenge Zone

The comparison is total margin per hour: margin on one times how many you could make. It usually favors the faster cheaper product, which surprises students who assumed the expensive one was better.

Week 17Score It With Real People

Building · sits beside: Collecting and interpreting data (5.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

3.5. 28 divided by 8.

The point

Numbers, not impressions. Eight people, three products, and a column for what they would actually buy.

Answers and marking
  • Averages are the total divided by 8.
  • The highest average and the most 'would buy' votes are often different products. Where they differ, the buy column is the one to trust.
Watch for

Students explain the products first, which contaminates every score. The rule is: say the price and nothing else until the person stops talking.

Challenge Zone

A high score with no purchases usually means priced too high, or it is nice but they do not need it. Require one reason about price and one that is not.

Week 18Cut One

Building · sits beside: Comparing decimals and fractions (5.NBT.3, 5.NF.2)
Warm-up answer

Equal. 7/2 is 3.5.

The point

Cut the weakest product deliberately, on the week 16 and 17 numbers, so the other two get the time.

Answers and marking
  • Margin per minute is margin divided by minutes to make. It is the fairest single comparison and usually decides it.
  • Two reasons from two different columns is the requirement, so a student cannot cut on score alone.
Watch for

Students keep all three because cutting feels like failing. Frame it as buying time for the other two, which is exactly what it is.

Challenge Zone

If no evidence would change their mind, they are not deciding on the numbers. Saying so plainly is the point of the question.

Week 19Your All-In Cost

Money · sits beside: All four operations with decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$0.70. $8.40 divided by 24 is $0.35, times 2.

The point

Every material divided down, plus packaging, plus waste. To the penny, on the student's own products.

Answers and marking
  • Waste line: cost of 10 divided by 9 sellable.
  • Most students are 20 to 40 cents out from their week 15 guess, and almost always low.
Watch for

Packaging is the most commonly missed line, then waste, then anything borrowed. Ask each team to name three costs not on their sheet.

Challenge Zone

At 1 in 5 waste the cost of 10 is divided by 8 rather than 9, which raises the unit cost noticeably. The product hurt more is the one with the higher materials cost, because there is more money in each wasted one.

Week 20What It Costs Before You Sell Anything

Money · sits beside: Adding decimals; multi-step problems (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$13.70.

The point

The full fixed cost list, and what it does to the real cost of one item at different volumes.

Answers and marking
  • Fixed cost per item is total fixed divided by how many sold, so it falls as volume rises.
  • The week 15 price often fails at 10 sales and works at 50, which is the point of the table.
Watch for

Students leave off anything they already owned or borrowed. The test is whether it would cost money to replace.

Challenge Zone

The good sign has to bring in enough extra sales that the extra margin covers $9. At a $1.50 margin that is six extra sales. Whether they believe it is the honest part of the answer.

Week 21Pricing the Line

Money · sits beside: Operations with decimals; comparing (5.NBT.7, 5.NBT.3)
Warm-up answer

About $2.70. Cost $1.35 doubled gives a margin equal to the cost, which is half the price.

The point

Two prices that work as a pair. The better product must be clearly more expensive and the gap must be big enough to notice.

Answers and marking
  • Margin is price minus all-in cost. Share of price is margin divided by price.
  • Any price at or below the real cost of one must be stopped here.
Watch for

Students price the cheap one first and then have no room above it. Price the better product first and set the cheaper one clearly below.

Challenge Zone

If the prices are close, most buyers take the better one. Whether that is bad depends on the margins: if the better product also has the better margin, close prices are actively good. Require the arithmetic.

Week 22Your Break-Even, In Units

Money · sits beside: Dividing decimals; rounding up in context (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

12. $21.00 divided by $1.75.

The point

Their own break-even number, rounded up, checked against how many people will actually walk past.

Answers and marking
  • Average margin across two products is the mean of the two margins, which is an approximation and worth saying so.
  • Rounding up because a partial sale does not exist and because rounding down leaves fixed costs uncovered.
Watch for

Students divide by price rather than margin. Same error as week 5 and it comes back here, so check it again.

Challenge Zone

Halfway is half the break-even number, rounded up. If session one lands well below it the realistic responses are to change the price, bring more of the product that sold, or accept a loss. All three are legitimate.

Week 23The Forecast

Money · sits beside: Multiplying and adding decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$89.00. (18 x $3.00) + (7 x $5.00).

The point

Written before anything is sold, in pen, and not changed afterwards. This is the page that makes week 31 mean anything.

Answers and marking
  • The three checks are the assessment: above break even, below an even share, and physically makeable in the time available.
  • A forecast failing any check must be changed now, not defended later.
Watch for

Students want to revise the forecast after session one. Do not allow it. Photograph or initial the page if that helps.

Challenge Zone

Forecasts run high because people forecast the day they are hoping for, and because they count everyone who walks past as a possible customer. Students who lower their forecast for a stated reason have done the exercise.

Week 24The Business Plan

Review · sits beside: Review: decimals, fractions, division (5.NBT.7, 5.NF.1, 5.NBT.6)
Warm-up answer

$49.50. 30 x $2.25 = $67.50, minus $18.00 fixed.

The point

The whole of trimester two on one page, with numbers copied from the file rather than remembered.

Answers and marking
  • A blank line is diagnostic, not a failure. It shows what is still to do.
  • The three risks section is what makes it a plan rather than a wish.
Watch for

Students copy a wrong number forward from an earlier week. Have them redo the forecast profit arithmetic here from scratch as a check.

Challenge Zone

At half the forecast, many plans fall below zero. The number that matters is then the break-even figure, and it belongs on the plan in place of the optimistic one.

Trimester 3 · Weeks 25 to 36

Run it twice

Sell, measure, change one thing, sell again. This is the difference between Grade 5 and Grade 4, and it turns the whole trimester into a controlled experiment.

Week 25Your Stall and Your Story

Selling · sits beside: Volume and area for packing and layout (5.MD.3, 5.MD.5)
Warm-up answer

6,000 cubic centimeters. 30 x 20 x 10.

The point

The stall planned to scale and the stock packed to fit, before the morning it has to happen.

Answers and marking
  • Table area for 120 cm by 60 cm is 7,200 square centimeters.
  • How many fit in the box is box volume divided by product volume, and it is always an overestimate because things do not pack perfectly.
Watch for

Students plan the layout in their head. Require the drawing to scale; the discovery that it does not fit belongs this week, not on the day.

Challenge Zone

The 28 not on display are the restock. The rule to write is at what number left on the table somebody puts more out, and it should be well before empty.

Week 26Signs and Prices People Can See

Selling · sits beside: Area; converting units (5.MD.1, 5.NF.4)
Warm-up answer

1,350 square centimeters. 45 x 30.

The point

Four things on the sign, both prices readable from six feet, and small price cards doing a different job from the big sign.

Answers and marking
  • The six-foot test almost always sends the prices back to be made bigger.
  • The price card's job is to answer the question at the moment of choosing, when the customer is looking at the product and not at the sign.
Watch for

Students write sentences. Count the words out loud; more than about twelve and it is a leaflet.

Challenge Zone

A 60 by 45 sheet is 2,700 square centimeters. Any layout with the arithmetic shown is acceptable; the point is noticing that offcuts are money.

Week 27The Pitch, With Numbers

Selling · sits beside: Reporting on a topic with facts (SL.5.4)
Warm-up answer

About 2 minutes 15 seconds each, or 135 seconds. 90 minutes divided by 40.

The point

Five sentences, one of which contains a real measured number rather than an adjective.

Answers and marking
  • The number must come from something they measured: minutes to make, how long it lasts, or what the week 17 testers said.
  • Timing three attempts shows visible improvement inside one sitting.
Watch for

Students invent a number that sounds good. Ask 'how do you know that' every time, which is also the Challenge Zone question.

Challenge Zone

The answer to 'how do you know' has to name the measurement and when it was taken. A student who cannot answer it should change the number, not the answer.

Week 28Inventory and Prep

Running It · sits beside: Multiplication and division with decimals (5.NBT.6, 5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

About 20. 60% of 34 is 20.4.

The point

Everything counted before the session, because opening inventory is half of every calculation in week 30.

Answers and marking
  • Opening inventory must be counted twice and both counts recorded.
  • The float is the change to serve the first customer, and teams forget it every year.
Watch for

Teams count roughly. A rough opening count makes the week 30 reconciliation impossible, so insist on the second count.

Challenge Zone

The restocking rule should trigger well before the table is empty. A stall that looks picked over sells more slowly than one that looks full, which is worth saying out loud.

Week 29Session One

Running It · sits beside: Recording and interpreting data (5.MD.2, 5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

Open. The forecast is copied from week 23 before selling starts.

The point

The session itself. This page is a recording instrument, not homework.

Answers and marking
  • The tally must be marked live and in the right product row.
  • The traffic count is what makes week 35's fair comparison possible, so it cannot be skipped.
Watch for

Teams forget the traffic count once it gets busy. Assign it to one person whose only job it is, or pause the market for thirty seconds at each mark.

Challenge Zone

A slow stretch with high traffic is a selling problem. A slow stretch with low traffic is a timing problem. Different fixes, and students who separate them are doing real analysis.

Week 30Count It Up, By Product

Money · sits beside: All four operations with decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

$79.50. (19 x $2.50) + (8 x $4.00).

The point

One line per product, then fixed costs, then a check against the money box and the inventory.

Answers and marking
  • Profit so far is total margin minus total fixed costs, and it is often negative after one session. That is expected with two sessions to cover the fixed costs.
  • Opening minus closing should equal the tally. It usually does not, by a little.
Watch for

Students report money in as profit. Hold the distinction; it is the same error as Grade 4 week 32 and it survives into adulthood.

Challenge Zone

Three real reasons the counts disagree: a sale marked in the wrong row, a sale not marked at all while busy, and a miscount at opening. Only the last is carelessness rather than the normal friction of a busy table.

Week 31Forecast Against Actual

Money · sits beside: Subtracting decimals; interpreting differences (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

7 under. 25 forecast, 18 actual.

The point

The forecast opened and compared. Nearly all of this year's learning is in this one table.

Answers and marking
  • Difference as a share of the forecast is what makes the rows comparable. Being 5 out on a forecast of 8 is far worse than 5 out on a forecast of 50.
  • Two different reasons for the worst row is the requirement. Both can be true.
Watch for

Students explain the gap by blaming the day. Push for at least one reason that is about a decision they made.

Challenge Zone

A perfect forecast from a first-time forecaster is luck. Reliable accuracy comes from having been wrong and adjusted, which is why the exercise needs the forecast to have been written in pen.

Week 32What the Numbers Say

Money · sits beside: Interpreting data; fractions of a quantity (5.MD.2, 5.NF.6)
Warm-up answer

2/3. 18 out of 27.

The point

Slice the session four ways and find one finding with a number in it.

Answers and marking
  • The by-product slice usually has the clearest story and points at week 33.
  • The finding must be a sentence containing a number. 'It did not sell well' is not a finding.
Watch for

Students write findings without numbers. Reject those and hand them back; the discipline is the lesson.

Challenge Zone

Slices can disagree, and the resolution is usually that they are measuring different things. Students who find a way for both to be true are doing genuinely good reasoning.

Week 33Change One Thing

Running It · sits beside: Multi-step problems; predicting with decimals (5.NBT.7)
Warm-up answer

Less, by $2.20. Before: 20 x $1.80 = $36.00. After: the margin falls to $1.30 and 26 sell, so 26 x $1.30 = $33.80. More sales, less money, which is the point of the question.

The point

One change, chosen from the week 32 finding, with a written prediction. Everything else stays the same, which is what makes session two readable.

Answers and marking
  • The change must trace to a measurement, not to a preference.
  • 'We will know it worked if' has to be something they can actually observe in session two.
Watch for

Teams want to change three things because all three seem good. Hold the line at one. The point of the year is being able to attribute the result.

Challenge Zone

Sales per person who walked past controls for a busier session. Students who reach that on their own have understood the difference between a result and a fair comparison.

Week 34Session Two

Running It · sits beside: Recording and interpreting data (5.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

Open. Copied from the week 33 forecast before selling starts.

The point

The second session, measured exactly the same way as the first.

Answers and marking
  • Traffic must be counted the same way or week 35 cannot compare them.
  • Customer reactions to the specific change are the qualitative half of the evidence.
Watch for

Teams measure session two more casually because they are tired of counting. The comparison is worthless if the two measurements were taken differently.

Challenge Zone

A change can work while the session is still worse, if something else got worse at the same time. Naming that in advance stops a wrong conclusion in week 35.

Week 35Did It Work?

Review · sits beside: Comparing and subtracting decimals (5.NBT.7, 5.NF.2)
Warm-up answer

Session one. 27/60 is 0.45; 31/80 is about 0.39.

The point

The two sessions compared fairly, using sales per person rather than raw sales, and an honest verdict.

Answers and marking
  • Sales per person is sales divided by traffic.
  • 'We cannot tell' is a legitimate verdict and should be accepted with a reason.
Watch for

Students conclude the change worked because session two sold more. If more people came, that is not evidence. This is the single most important correction in the year.

Challenge Zone

One session is not proof. Any answer above about three sessions with reasoning is good. What matters is that they see a single result as weak evidence rather than as an answer.

Week 36Your Numbers Story

Review · sits beside: Review; writing an opinion supported by facts (W.5.1)
Warm-up answer

Open. Compare with what the student wrote in week 1.

The point

The whole year told with the numbers still attached.

Answers and marking
  • Every line traces to a page of the Numbers File. A student who cannot fill the page has an incomplete file, which is itself worth knowing.
  • The three things they know now must each contain a number.
Watch for

Students write the good parts. The 'number that surprised you most' line is required, not optional.

Challenge Zone

What a business that ran twice knows is whether a change did anything, which a business that ran once cannot know about itself. A third session would start to separate a real effect from a good day.
