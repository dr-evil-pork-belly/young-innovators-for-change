Teacher Guide

The Venture Year · Grade 4 · 36 weeks

Answers, the point of each week, and the specific wrong turn each one produces. Written for a teacher who has never run an enterprise program and does not intend to become an expert in one.

Where a question is a judgment call this guide says so rather than inventing a correct answer to grade against. Roughly a third of the year is open by design.

Market day, and the rule about money

Week 31The class runs a market. Real products, real customers, real decisions. Play money.
Set these twoThe startup fund (the same for every student, around $20 in play money works) and a price list for the class supply table. Keep supply prices near the real cost of the materials so the arithmetic stays honest.
The loopBank lends the fund → student buys materials from the supply table → student sells and makes their own change → student repays the fund. What is left is profit.
Why play moneyIt is what lets students run their own money box, so making change becomes a skill they practice rather than a risk you manage. It also fixes the money in the room, which is what makes the week 30 market-size arithmetic possible.
RecordsEach student keeps their own live tally in the workbook. Weeks 32 and 33 are built on it, so a tally filled in afterwards corrupts two weeks of arithmetic.
Trimester 1 · Weeks 1 to 12

See it

Students study businesses other people run. Nothing of their own is designed until week 13, on purpose: they should be able to describe how a business works before they try to build one.

Week 1Every Business Is a Trade

Value · sits beside: Multi-step word problems (4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

$8. Four packs at $3 is $12; $20 minus $12 is $8.

The point

A business is a voluntary trade. Both sides have to want it, which is why nobody can be made to buy anything and why a business that stops being worth the money simply stops.

Answers and grading
  • Find the trade: seller gives a cup of hot chocolate and gets $2; buyer gives $2 and gets something warm to drink. Accept any wording that gets both directions right.
  • The table of four trades is observational. Anything real counts, including trades at school with no money in them if the student explains the swap.
Watch for

Students write only the seller's side. Push them to fill in what the buyer hands over, because the whole idea collapses if the trade only goes one way.

Challenge Zone

A present is not a trade in the business sense: nothing comes back and the giver expects nothing. Strong answers notice that something does come back (thanks, the relationship) but that it is not something you could price. Both conclusions are fine if argued.

Week 2Needs and Wants

Value · sits beside: Money word problems (4.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

Yes. $2.75 + $1.40 = $4.15, leaving 85 cents.

The point

Needs and wants both make real businesses, but they behave differently when money gets tight. Wants are cut first.

Answers and grading
  • Clear needs: bread, toothpaste, a winter coat. Clear wants: a phone case, a video game, concert tickets. The arguable two are shoes (which shoes?) and a birthday cake. Accept either placement with a reason.
  • Price test: water is the stronger answer, because at a hot fair with no fountain it is close to a need and candy has plenty of substitutes. Accept candy with a reason (it costs more to make, it is a treat). Grade the reason, which should be about the situation, not the object. That idea comes back in week 6.
Watch for

The sorting activity turns into an argument about whether phones are a need. Let it run for two minutes, then use it: the disagreement is the lesson.

Challenge Zone

A needs business faces far more competition, because everyone already sells bread. Good answers reach 'more buyers but more sellers'.

Week 3Problems Worth Solving

Value · sits beside: Multi-step word problems (4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

2 packs. 28 x 3 = 84 sheets; 84 / 50 = 1.68, so 2 packs.

The point

Businesses start from an annoyance somebody actually had, not from a clever idea generated on demand.

Answers and grading
  • Open. There is no wrong annoyance. The only weak answers are abstract ones ('pollution'), which cannot be acted on by a nine-year-old.
  • Starred problems must annoy at least ten other people. Make students name some of the ten rather than asserting it.
Watch for

Students reach for enormous problems because they sound impressive. Redirect to the walk to school, the lunch line, the bottom of a backpack.

Challenge Zone

A problem that annoys a few people a lot is usually easier to sell to, because those people are already looking for a fix. A problem that mildly annoys everybody has no urgency behind it. Either answer earns credit with reasoning; this one is genuinely contested among adults.

Week 4Who Is It For?

Customers · sits beside: Money word problems (4.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

$9 each, then $6.75 each. It falls by $2.25.

The point

'Everyone' is not an audience. Narrowing the customer makes every later decision easier.

Answers and grading
  • Cookies at 3 pm: roughly 8 to 11 years old, money from a parent or an allowance, in a hurry, competing with the corner store and with going straight home.
  • The five-minute count is real data. Students who invent it will produce suspiciously round numbers.
Watch for

Students name a group and think they have finished. Ask 'which ones?' twice and the answer gets useful.

Challenge Zone

Narrow gains fit and word of mouth; it gives up volume. Both halves needed for full credit.

Week 5Ask a Better Question

Customers · sits beside: Asking questions and building on answers (SL.4.1)
Warm-up answer

16 said yes, 8 said no.

The point

People guess about the future and remember the past. Ask about the past.

Answers and grading
  • Rewrites: 'Do you like it?' becomes 'What do you use instead right now?'; 'Is $5 a good price?' becomes 'What did you pay last time you bought something like this?'
  • The three interviews are the assignment. Written answers should sound like speech, not like a survey.
Watch for

Students ask friends, who say yes to everything. Insist on at least one person who is not a friend.

Challenge Zone

Two good reasons: they were being polite, and there is a difference between wanting something and wanting it enough to hand over money today. Strong answers name both.

Week 6Why Is It Worth That?

Value · sits beside: Adding and subtracting multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.4)
Warm-up answer

$19.

The point

Value is not a property of the object. It is what a particular person, in a particular situation, will give up for it.

Answers and grading
  • Water prices should rise across the four settings: kitchen (near zero), vending machine (about $1.50), hot fair (about $3), airport (about $4). Exact numbers do not matter; the direction does.
  • Saves time / effort / worry: examples might be a taxi, a dishwasher, insurance.
Watch for

Students say the fair price is 'whatever it costs to make plus a bit'. That is week 20's method and it is not what this week is about.

Challenge Zone

Two shops, same backpack, both sell out: different customers, different convenience, different beliefs about quality. Reject 'the $45 customers were stupid'.

Week 7Where the Money Goes

Money · sits beside: Adding and subtracting multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.4)
Warm-up answer

$39.

The point

Revenue is not profit. Money arrives already committed to the costs that produced it.

Answers and grading
  • Trace the money: revenue $60 (20 x $3), cost $18, stayed $42.
  • Costs: string and beads yes, cardboard sign yes. The money customers paid is not a cost. The borrowed table and already-owned scissors are the arguable two.
Watch for

Students count the money customers paid as a cost because it appears in the list. Ask which direction it moved.

Challenge Zone

The borrowed table was not free: it cost the ability to keep it, and it cost whatever happens when it is taken back. This is opportunity cost, arrived at without the term.

Week 8Cost and Price

Money · sits beside: Multiplying multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.5)
Warm-up answer

$8.40. 24 x 35 cents.

The point

Cost is set by the world; price is chosen by you and judged by the customer. The gap between them is the business.

Answers and grading
  • Gaps: bracelet $2.10, dog treat bag $2.80, card price $2.00.
  • The cookie pricing is open, but a price below 40 cents should be challenged immediately.
Watch for

Students price at cost, or below it, because they want to be fair. Use the 'sell 100 of them' question: losing money faster is not fairness.

Challenge Zone

10 at $5 keeps $41.00; 30 at $2 keeps $33.00. So the higher price wins on this arithmetic, but 30 sales is more customers, more practice and more word of mouth. Both choices defensible; the arithmetic is not.

Week 9Profit: What Is Left

Money · sits beside: Multi-step word problems (4.OA.3, 4.NBT.4)
Warm-up answer

$22. Revenue $36 minus cost $14.

The point

REVENUE minus COST equals PROFIT, and profit can be negative. That is a result, not a disgrace.

Answers and grading
  • Three days: Monday $15, Tuesday $0, Wednesday minus $8.
  • Popcorn profit $42; slushies profit $8. Both 'made $60' and the statement is useless.
Watch for

Students refuse to write a negative number, or write it without the minus sign. Insist. It is the first time many of them have met one in a real context.

Challenge Zone

$8 over six hours is about $1.33 an hour. A student who calls that bad should say what would change their mind: enjoying it, learning, a first attempt, or a better Saturday next time.

Week 10Goods and Services

Value · sits beside: Multiplying multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.5)
Warm-up answer

$84.

The point

A good can be made ahead and sold later. A service is bounded by the hours one person has.

Answers and grading
  • Goods: lemonade, bracelets, cards. Services: raking leaves, tutoring, feeding a cat.
  • Dog walker: $72 max at $12 for 6 hours; $90 at $15; more than that only if someone else walks dogs too. The limit on a cookie seller is materials, money and how many people come past.
Watch for

Students classify tutoring as a good because 'you get worksheets'. Ask what they are actually paying for.

Challenge Zone

Services are hard because the business stops entirely when the person stops. Illness, vacations and school all take the whole business offline.

Week 11Why People Choose

Customers · sits beside: Adding and subtracting multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.4)
Warm-up answer

7 more.

The point

You are never the only option, and 'buy nothing' is always on the list.

Answers and grading
  • The ranking is opinion; the tally of five real people is not. Compare the two and the gap is the lesson.
  • Whatever wins the tally, compare it with each student's own ranking on page 1.
Watch for

Students assume cheapest always wins. The survey is there to test that belief against five real answers.

Challenge Zone

People pay more for convenience, reliability, brand, ethics, or how it makes them feel. Two named examples with what the extra money buys.

Week 12The Business Detective

Review · sits beside: Review: word problems and money (4.OA.3, 4.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

$72. Revenue $120 minus cost $48.

The point

Everything from weeks 1 to 11 applied to one real business the student can actually see.

Answers and grading
  • Entirely dependent on the business chosen. Grade the reasoning, not the numbers. An estimate marked with a '?' and defended beats a confident invention.
Watch for

Students guess cost and price and present them as fact. Require the question mark on anything not observed.

Challenge Zone

Once rent, power and the owner's own time are added, most small businesses look far less profitable. Students who reach 'the owner is paying themselves last' have understood something real.

Trimester 2 · Weeks 13 to 24

Build it

Each student picks one problem and carries it all the way to a one-page plan. This is where the Grade 4 multiplication and division standards do the most work.

Week 13Your Turn: Pick a Problem

Making · sits beside: Multi-step word problems (4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

14. Four cost $7.00, so twelve cost $21.00; two more make $24.50, leaving 50 cents, which is not enough for another.

The point

Commit to a problem, not to a product. The product will change; the problem should not.

Answers and grading
  • Open. The scoring is a decision tool, not a grading rubric.
  • The problem statement must name who, what and why. Reject anything that starts 'I want to sell'.
Watch for

Students arrive with a product already decided and reverse-engineer a problem to fit. The honesty check on page 2 exists for exactly this; do not skip it.

Challenge Zone

Finding an existing solution is good news, not bad. What is still wrong with it is the opening.

Week 14Your Customer

Customers · sits beside: Equivalent fractions (4.NF.1)
Warm-up answer

6/10 = 3/5. Six tenths and three fifths are the same amount.

The point

One named person beats a demographic. Every later decision gets easier.

Answers and grading
  • Open, but 'kids' or 'everyone' should be sent back.
  • The three interviews must be with real people and recorded as spoken.
Watch for

The customer is described as the student themselves. Sometimes true and fine, but make them say so deliberately.

Challenge Zone

The user and the payer are often different: children want, parents pay. Two different messages needed. This is a genuinely advanced idea and worth praising when it lands.

Week 15Ten Ideas, Then One

Making · sits beside: Patterns and rules (4.OA.5)
Warm-up answer

3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23; tenth is 39.

The point

Produce a pile of ideas before choosing. Idea nine is often ideas three and seven combined.

Answers and grading
  • Ten is the requirement. Students who stop at four have not done the exercise; the last few are where the interesting ones appear.
Watch for

Students self-censor and write only 'sensible' ideas. Explicitly permit silly ones and the count comes easily.

Challenge Zone

Defending the worst idea teaches that ideas are not good or bad in the abstract, only right or wrong for a customer.

Week 16Build a Rough One

Making · sits beside: Area and perimeter (4.MD.3)
Warm-up answer

Area 108 sq in; perimeter 42 in.

The point

A rough prototype made fast is more useful than a polished one made slowly, because you are still willing to hear criticism of it.

Answers and grading
  • Area and perimeter must be correct for whatever the student drew. This is the week 4.MD.3 gets real practice.
  • 'What went wrong that you did not expect' is the most valuable line on the page.
Watch for

Students spend the week making it beautiful and then cannot bear the feedback in week 17. Set a time limit out loud.

Challenge Zone

Two deliberately different versions produce better feedback than two similar ones. The reason for the preference matters more than the preference.

Week 17Show It to Someone

Customers · sits beside: Word problems with whole numbers (4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

Total 35; the middle score is 8.

The point

Show it, then be quiet. Everything they misunderstand is something a customer would also misunderstand.

Answers and grading
  • The signal and opinion split is the point: anything said twice is a signal.
  • Five ratings should be plotted on the blank bar chart.
Watch for

Students explain and defend, which destroys the data. Give them a rule: they may say only the opening line and then nothing until the person stops talking.

Challenge Zone

Deciding whether the one dissenter matters depends on whether they are your customer. A non-customer's dislike is not a signal. Students who ask 'were they the sort of person I am building for?' understand it.

Week 18Make It Better

Making · sits beside: Multi-step word problems (4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

17 minutes each; 102 minutes for six.

The point

Change it because of evidence, and deliberately decide what not to change.

Answers and grading
  • Three changes must trace to week 17 signals, not to preference.
  • 'What got worse' should not be blank. Nearly every change costs something.
Watch for

Students change everything and lose what people liked. The 'not changing' box is as important as the changes.

Challenge Zone

The trade-off is real: better and slower against faster and rougher. Force the arithmetic before the opinion.

Week 19What It Costs to Make One

Money · sits beside: Multiplying multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.5)
Warm-up answer

6 bracelets per bag (50 / 8 = 6 remainder 2); beads cost 64 cents a bracelet (8 cents x 8). Also accept 67 cents ($4.00 / 6, rounded) from a student who says the 2 leftover beads are wasted; that is the spoilage idea on page 2.

The point

Unit cost, not total cost. Packs must be divided down before they are multiplied back up.

Answers and grading
  • The worked example is done for them; their own table is the assessment.
  • Check that packaging and waste are on the list. Both are easy to leave off.
Watch for

Students divide by the wrong number, or skip the division and guess. Do the cost-per-bead step on the board once.

Challenge Zone

With one in ten wasted, ten items cost $8.00 but only nine are sellable, so the real cost is about 89 cents each. Students who find this have met spoilage honestly.

Week 20Choosing Your Price

Money · sits beside: Money problems, multi-digit multiplication (4.MD.2, 4.NBT.5)
Warm-up answer

$69.

The point

Price is the one number the student controls. Three methods, then a decision.

Answers and grading
  • The three methods will disagree. Students must choose and justify, not average them.
  • Any price at or below the week 19 cost must be stopped and fixed here.
Watch for

Students price low because they are afraid nobody will buy. Ask what happens at 100 sales.

Challenge Zone

$4.99 works on perception but is a nuisance in coins at a school market. Students who mention the line have thought about operations.

Week 21Profit on One Sale

Money · sits beside: Adding and subtracting multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.4)
Warm-up answer

$2.30 on one; $27.60 on twelve.

The point

The student's own price minus their own cost. This is the number the year has been building toward.

Answers and grading
  • Scale-up table is arithmetic and should be graded.
  • 'How many must I sell' is division and often produces an uncomfortable number. That discomfort is the point.
Watch for

A negative profit here means weeks 19 or 20 need revisiting. Do not let it stand and do not let the student feel it is a failure.

Challenge Zone

Raising the price by 50 cents and selling a quarter fewer (15 instead of 20) wins only when profit on one sale is under $1.50, because 15 x (profit + 50 cents) beats 20 x profit only then. With the warm-up numbers ($2.30 on one), 20 sales make $46.00 and 15 sales at the higher price make $42.00, so the higher price loses. Require the arithmetic both ways with the student's own numbers.

Week 22How Many Can You Make?

Making · sits beside: Division with remainders (4.NBT.6, 4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

17, with 1 minute left over. 120 / 7 = 17 remainder 1.

The point

Two limits, money and time. The smaller one is the real plan.

Answers and grading
  • Timing must be measured, not estimated.
  • The money limit is the startup fund divided by the cost to make one. Tell students the fund amount this week (see week 29), or they cannot fill that row.
  • The batch timing decides whether batching is faster for this product.
Watch for

Students plan to make 200 of something that takes 12 minutes each. The capacity table stops this before market day does.

Challenge Zone

Teaching costs 30 of your minutes and buys 90 minutes of the friend's work (their 2 hours minus the 30 spent learning), or 120 if the teaching comes first. If the friend works as fast as you, accepting always wins: 90 minutes of their making against 30 of yours. It loses only if they are about three times slower than you or worse, or if one item takes longer than their whole shift, so they finish nothing.

Week 23A Name and a Look

Telling · sits beside: Area and perimeter (4.MD.3)
Warm-up answer

16. Four across by four down.

The point

A name is a promise. Say what it is, be memorable, be sayable.

Answers and grading
  • The listener's guess is the real test of the name, not the student's own opinion of it.
  • Label arithmetic: area and how many fit a 12 by 8 sheet.
Watch for

Students pick a pun nobody understands. The guess test settles it without an argument.

Challenge Zone

A label size dividing evenly into 12 by 8 (2x2, 3x2, 4x2, 6x4) wastes nothing. Checking the name still fits is the part they forget.

Week 24Your One-Page Plan

Review · sits beside: Review: cost, price, profit, capacity (4.OA.3, 4.NBT.5, 4.NBT.6)
Warm-up answer

$78. Profit per item $2.60 x 30.

The point

Everything from weeks 13 to 23 on one page an adult can read in two minutes.

Answers and grading
  • Blank lines are diagnostic, not failures. They show what is still to do.
  • The three risks section is what turns this from a wish into a plan.
Watch for

Students copy across without re-checking, so an early wrong number survives to market day. Have them redo the profit arithmetic here from scratch.

Challenge Zone

Selling only half is the realistic case. If half still clears zero, the plan is sound. If not, the break-even number is the one that matters and it should go on the plan.

Trimester 3 · Weeks 25 to 36

Run it

Getting ready, market day in week 31, and then the honest arithmetic of whether it made any money.

Week 25What Do You Say First?

Telling · sits beside: Writing an opinion with reasons (W.4.1)
Warm-up answer

30 minutes. 45 seconds x 40 = 1800 seconds.

The point

The first sentence has one job: make somebody stop. It is about them, not about you.

Answers and grading
  • Rewrites should shift from 'I made' to 'do you'. Questions work best.
  • The three lines must be tested on real people; 'did they stop' is the measurement.
Watch for

Students write a paragraph. Cap it at one sentence out loud.

Challenge Zone

A trick line works once and then costs trust. Good answers name the second encounter.

Week 26Make Your Sign

Telling · sits beside: Area and perimeter (4.MD.3)
Warm-up answer

616 square inches.

The point

Four elements, readable at six feet in three seconds.

Answers and grading
  • Area and perimeter of their own sign. The grid is to scale: 11 squares of 2 inches is 22 inches wide, 7 squares is 14 inches tall.
  • Whatever fails the six-foot test gets enlarged first.
Watch for

Students write sentences on the sign. Count the words out loud; over about twelve and it is a flyer, not a sign.

Challenge Zone

Cutting a 22 by 28 sheet with minimal waste is a real optimization problem. Accept any layout with the arithmetic shown.

Week 27The 60-Second Pitch

Telling · sits beside: Reporting on a topic, speaking clearly (SL.4.4)
Warm-up answer

30 times.

The point

Four sentences in a fixed order: problem, what I made, who and how much, why me.

Answers and grading
  • The order matters. A pitch that starts with the product skips the reason anyone should care.
  • Timing three attempts shows improvement inside one sitting.
Watch for

Students merge sentences two and three and lose the price. Make them count four.

Challenge Zone

Handling an interruption without restarting is the advanced skill. Answer the question, then return to the sentence you were on.

Week 28Practice, Then Practice

Telling · sits beside: Speaking clearly at an understandable pace (SL.4.4)
Warm-up answer

36 times; 36 minutes.

The point

Repetition frees attention for the customer. Twenty repetitions is the book's target.

Answers and grading
  • The log is the assignment. Ask students to compare how the first and the twentieth felt.
  • The three hard responses should be written before they are needed, not improvised at the table.
Watch for

Students practice silently in their heads, which does not work. Out loud, to a person or at minimum to a wall.

Challenge Zone

'Too expensive' has two different meanings and two different answers. Students who write only a discount have missed it.

Week 29The Class Bank

Running It · sits beside: Solving money problems, making change (4.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

$2.25: one dollar, one dollar, one quarter. Counting up from $2.75.

The point

The closed play economy: the bank lends a startup fund, students buy materials with it, sell for play money, and repay the fund. What is left is profit.

Answers and grading
  • Change: $1.50 for $3.50 from $5.00 (one dollar, two quarters); $5.20 for $4.80 from $10.00 (five dollars, two dimes).
  • The four steps are printed in order. Ask why repaying has to come last: the fund is only free to repay once the selling is done.
  • The three rules should be written in the student's own words, not copied.
Watch for

Two things to set before this lesson: the startup fund amount (the same for every student) and the price list for the class supply table. Keep supply prices close to the real cost of the materials so the arithmetic stays honest. A fund of around $20 in play money is a workable size.

Challenge Zone

If you spend the whole fund and sell half of what you made, whether you can repay depends entirely on your margin. At a price of twice cost, selling half gets you back exactly to the fund and no further. Students who reach that are doing break-even reasoning without the term.

Week 30Market Day Plan

Running It · sits beside: Time intervals and money (4.MD.2, 4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

150 minutes; roughly one person every 2 and a half minutes.

The point

Plan the day in enough detail that nothing has to be decided on the day.

Answers and grading
  • The bring list is the assessment. Anything missing on the day traces back to this page. The money box and starting change are the newest line; check that every list has them.
  • The three what-ifs are the difference between a plan and a hope.
Watch for

Students leave the what-ifs blank or write 'it will be fine'. Selling out, nobody coming and rain each need a real answer.

Challenge Zone

Market size, and the reason play money earns its place. Tell students the shopper allowance and the expected number of shoppers. Allowance times shoppers is all the money there is; divided by the number of stalls is an even share. A student planning to take three times an even share has just learned something about their forecast that no amount of encouragement would have taught them. With real money none of this is knowable.

Week 31Market Day

Running It · sits beside: Money and time on the day (4.MD.2)
Warm-up answer

The prediction is written before the day and compared after. That comparison is the assessment.

The point

The day itself. The workbook page is a recording instrument, not homework.

Answers and grading
  • The tally must be marked live. A tally reconstructed afterwards is a guess and the arithmetic in weeks 32 and 33 inherits the error.
  • Five quotations in customers' own words, including from people who did not buy.
Watch for

Students forget to tally once it gets busy. Assign a partner to prompt, or pause the market for 30 seconds halfway.

Challenge Zone

Prediction against reality is the single most useful reflection of the year. Over or under, the size of the gap is the finding.

Week 32Count It Up

Money · sits beside: Adding and subtracting multi-digit numbers (4.NBT.4)
Warm-up answer

$81. (17 x 3) + (6 x 5) = 51 + 30.

The point

Revenue, worked out properly and checked twice. Still not profit.

Answers and grading
  • Multiple prices need a row each. Adding a second time in a different order catches adding mistakes.
  • Planned against actual is where week 24's optimism gets measured.
Watch for

Students announce revenue as though it were profit. Hold the line until week 33.

Challenge Zone

Average sale and the effect of three more customers shows how sensitive a small stall is to a handful of people.

Week 33Did You Make a Profit?

Money · sits beside: Multi-step word problems (4.OA.3)
Warm-up answer

$38. $84 minus ($31 + $6 + $9).

The point

Every cost, including the sign and the wasted ones. Then the honest number, checked a second way against the money box.

Answers and grading
  • Check that the sign, anything wasted and anything borrowed are on the list. Wasted materials belong in the first line only, so they are not counted twice.
  • The two methods must agree: revenue minus cost, and money-in-box minus the startup fund minus any cost not paid out of the box (something still to replace, a sign made from home materials). When they disagree the tally and the box disagree, which is exactly the check a real business runs at the end of a day.
  • Profit per hour can be startlingly low. Do not soften it.
Watch for

Students omit costs that were paid before market day. Point back to week 7: did money leave your hands because of this business?

Challenge Zone

Doubling profit can need more sales than the day's foot traffic allowed. If it does, that constraint is a real finding, not a failure.

Week 34What Would You Change?

Review · sits beside: Fractions of a whole number (4.NF.4)
Warm-up answer

33. Half of 22 is 11, and 22 + 11 = 33.

The point

Separate decisions from luck. Only decisions can be reviewed.

Answers and grading
  • The bar comparison uses their own week 24 and week 32 numbers.
  • 'What went wrong that was my decision' is the line that matters.
Watch for

Students blame the weather, the location, other stalls. Some of that is fair. Insist on at least one item in the decision column.

Challenge Zone

Arguing the opposite case tests whether the conclusion was reasoned or assumed.

Week 35What to Do With Profit

Value · sits beside: Equivalent fractions and adding fractions (4.NF.1, 4.NF.3)
Warm-up answer

$20, $10 and $10.

The point

Spend, reinvest or give. Reinvestment is how a business grows, and it is a real fractions exercise.

Answers and grading
  • The three fractions must sum to one whole. Halves become quarters first (4.NF.1), then the quarters add (4.NF.3): 2/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 4/4.
  • The reinvestment table shows the compounding loop concretely.
Watch for

Students split without checking the fractions add up. Require the working.

Challenge Zone

Risk appetite depends on what else the money is for and how confident they are that it will sell. Two named pieces of information.

Week 36Your Business Story

Review · sits beside: Review; writing an opinion with reasons (W.4.1)
Warm-up answer

Open. Compare with what the student wrote in week 1.

The point

  • Every line traces to a page of the Venture File. If a student cannot fill the page, the file is incomplete and that is worth knowing.
  • 'Would you do it again' is genuinely open. No is a respectable answer with a reason.
Watch for

Students write only the good parts. The biggest thing I got wrong is a required line, not an optional one.
